# Direct Play and external playback

Choose the preferred add action in **Settings → Prefer Direct Play**. The add sheet always offers both Download and Direct Play after the torrent file list is ready. Download continues to save selected files to Downloads/Webtor. Direct Play creates no full-sized video backing files.

Settings offers Auto RAM or a manual video cache of 64, 128, 256, 512 or 1,024 MiB per transfer. The same limit controls the download write-back cache. The saved choice applies to active and future transfers; lowering the limit lets normal piece replacement release excess buffers safely. Preview tasks retain their separate memory budget. Starting another Direct Play session releases the previous torrent's RAM session, while keeping its library entry, resume position and startup cache. Native readers are limited to four per Direct Play torrent. Settings also allows 8–500 peer connections per transfer, with 55 as the default.

**RAM cache slider** (Settings → Connections) sets the per-transfer cache from 16 MiB to 1 GiB, finer at the small end, applied when the slider is released; Auto stays a separate chip. A Direct Play torrent always keeps at least four of its pieces (the one playing, the next, one being verified and one arriving), so a setting smaller than that is raised for that torrent only instead of refusing it; only pieces over 256 MiB are refused. Three costs used to grow with the cache, so a smaller setting seeked faster: on Android the Go heap could reach about twice the cache before collecting (the engine now sets Go's memory limit to the open caches plus 256 MiB); every block written and every read rescanned a list of all cached pieces (now a constant-time LRU); and every peer refill listed every block of every missing piece in the window, tens of thousands with a large cache (now a peer stops once it has twice its request limit in free blocks, after all Now and Next pieces). **Auto RAM** chooses 64–512 MiB per transfer from Android's total and available memory, its low-memory threshold, and the number of active transfers. It reserves memory for Android and other work, and uses 64 MiB on low-RAM devices or under memory pressure. Auto is the default when no manual cache preference exists; existing manual choices are preserved. The app rechecks the budget on startup, on returning to the foreground, and before configuring a transfer. An active Direct Play torrent that needs larger pieces retains its working limit rather than accepting an unusable reduction. The external player can keep its own read buffer independently of this torrent cache.

Opening and container metadata pieces receive urgent priority. Direct Play saves only video metadata to internal storage: the first 2 MiB and last 2 MiB (rounded to pieces) of every selected video, which hold the MP4 moov or MKV header and cues, under `.startup`. The opened episode comes first in the plan, then the following episodes in file order. With no player open the engine fetches the rest at Normal priority in batches of a quarter of the RAM cache; beside playback a batch is the 5% playback reserve (at least one piece), below every playback window. Each piece is verified, written once and kept: switching episodes rewrites nothing. The cache is capped at 1 GiB overall; inactive torrents' caches are pruned oldest first. When a session attaches, the first 8 MiB of the plan is validated at once and other saved pieces are validated when their episode opens, so a large cache does not delay the start. `/play` validates the episode's saved pieces before raising its startup hints, so they are not fetched again. A validated piece replaces any partial copy a peer wrote into RAM (reads prefer RAM, so a player would otherwise read zeros inside the moov and some players crash) and is reported complete to the torrent library, which stops requesting it. All remaining video bytes stay in RAM. The asynchronous startup writer can hold up to 16 MiB of queued verified payload, retrying pieces skipped while it was full; peer buffers and engine metadata consume additional memory. A torrent whose pieces exceed one quarter of the RAM cache must use Download.

Warm startup pieces are checksum-validated before reuse, including torrent SHA-1 verification for v1 torrents. Validation and native piece hashing use the Go runtime’s available CPU allowance; the torrent client retains a shared cap on active hashers across torrents. Saved-data verification now pipelines independent pieces rather than awaiting them one at a time. Peer network I/O continues using Go's asynchronous runtime and the existing anacrolix scheduler.

The forward buffer adjusts to observed sequential read consumption and available RAM. Seeks retarget urgent pieces immediately. Idle descriptors park after 15 seconds and resume on the next read; in-flight reads are never parked. The playing descriptor (the latest past its open phase) is exempt: an external player stops reading while paused or once its own buffer is full, and that is when the engine fills the window ahead. Probes and the previous episode's descriptor still park. A blocked read can retry twice within a 45-second total wait, refreshing its urgent request through the current scheduler. This recovery is experimental: it does not add TorrServe's duplicate-block request algorithm. Sparse swarms and slow peers can still require buffering.

**Settings → Experimental → Adaptive buffer** is an optional feedback experiment, off by default. It adds look-ahead for variable external-player read demand using a smoothed mean and variance, then reduces that headroom as demand settles. Damping limits each adjustment and a dead band avoids small repeated resizes. The existing startup, seek and RAM-budget limits still apply, and the setting is captured when you press Play. The idea is inspired by feedback and scaling; it does not implement a mathematical universality theorem or promise faster swarm downloads. Correctness tests cover varying demand and real local-peer playback within a 16 MiB cache; public-swarm stall reduction has not been measured.

**Burst-aware buffer** is a separate opt-in experiment inspired by patterns across multiple timescales. It adds fast and slow demand estimates to the feedback controller so a short calm period does not immediately erase a longer burst history. It works independently and includes the Adaptive buffer feedback; with both switches on, the burst-aware policy is used. This is a practical multiscale heuristic, not a formal fractal estimator. Burst-aware buffer is on by default (Adaptive buffer is off; the two are exclusive in the app), and both apply to the next Play request. Default, adaptive, burst-aware and combined configurations are checked against the same local-peer playback and seek test with a 16 MiB RAM cache.

**Peer exploration** is an opt-in foraging-inspired heuristic for active transfers. Every ten seconds it samples connected peers' useful download and upload counters. When the connection limit is full and another discovered candidate is waiting, it can release one connection that has transferred no useful payload for at least 90 seconds. It preserves productive peers, new connections, the only connected peer and the sole known seed. At most one slot rotates per torrent every 30 seconds. The existing discovery, connection and dial limits remain in charge. The switch applies immediately, survives app restarts, and resets its observation history when changed. It is not a contextual-bandit learner and makes no throughput guarantee.

All four performance controls can be enabled together: Instant play boost, Adaptive buffer, Burst-aware buffer and Peer exploration. The combined local-peer test downloads and seeks through a 40 MiB payload with a 16 MiB cache while the peer-exploration policy is enabled and sampled. Burst-aware mode includes Adaptive feedback, boost enlarges the startup and maximum look-ahead targets, and peer exploration acts on connection slots; the storage budget remains the final limit. This verifies the tested combination's correctness, not a speedup on every network.

**Ghost mode** is an additional independent switch in Experimental, off by default, inspired by [Ghost Downloader's throughput-based auto acceleration](https://github.com/XiaoYouChR/Ghost-Downloader-3/blob/v3.8.3/features/http_pack/task.py#L277). Its torrent implementation is a feedback heuristic, not a trained neural model or HTTP range splitter. While discovered candidates are waiting and connection slots are available, it measures useful payload bytes over ten-second windows. After two stable windows it probes another ten peer dials per second, up to the existing 80/s ceiling. Two trial windows must show at least a 5% throughput gain to retain the increased rate; a decline over 20% aborts the trial early. Failed trials restore the previous rate and wait a minute before retrying. Idle transfers, full pools, changed active torrents, long sampling gaps, counter resets and approaching the user's download-rate cap discard the trial. Changes to transfer policy, connection limits, configured dialing or network route also reset it. The switch applies immediately, persists across restarts, and turning it off restores the latest configured dial rate (normally 30/s, or 80/s with Fast peer search).

Ghost mode leaves established connections, discovery sources, payload rate limits, piece priorities and RAM budgets unchanged. Fast peer search already selects the shared ceiling, so Ghost adds no dialing increase with that switch on. Adaptive and Burst-aware buffers retain their existing relationship; Ghost coordinates neither buffer policy nor peer replacement. A local-peer seek test covers Ghost alone and all five performance controls together with a 16 MiB cache. Trace-based policy tests exercise successful and failed probes, cooldowns, ceiling and invalidation behavior. This verifies bounded behavior and correctness, not faster playback or public-swarm performance.

**Ghost Pro mode** extends Ghost mode with bitfield awareness and smart swarm recycling. While running, it monitors each connected peer's piece bitfield against active torrent demand (uncompleted pieces in selected and streaming files). When connection limits are reached and candidate peers are waiting, Ghost Pro prioritizes evicting unproductive peers that possess zero pieces of our active selection (after 15 seconds idle), while protecting seeders and active data transfers. It automatically manages dial rate tuning and supersedes both basic Ghost mode and basic Peer exploration. When Ghost Pro mode is turned on, the conflicting Ghost mode and Peer exploration switches are automatically disabled and grayed out.

Ghost Pro's focused playback policy applies to both Download and Instant Play, through JNI and HTTP readers. Opening or seeking keeps the urgent request narrow (2 MiB, rounded to pieces) until verified bytes arrive; container head/tail probes remain urgent during opening. The player can begin as soon as its own startup requirements are met; the engine does not require the whole forward window to finish. Once opening is complete and the first read succeeds, Download uses an urgent span of at least 16 MiB or two pieces, a forward extent from the playhead of at least 64 MiB or six pieces (96 MiB minimum with Instant play boost), and another 32 MiB or four pieces of High-priority lookahead. Each tier uses the larger minimum. Seeks immediately move the path and release old reader priorities, preserving downloaded data and other readers' demand.

Instant Play fills its RAM budget around the playhead instead of using the Download window sizes. Active readers share about 95% of the cache; the remainder (at least one piece) holds a new reader's probe and pending startup saves. Each reader's share is counted in whole pieces, so an unaligned playhead cannot pin an extra piece, and opening or closing a reader resizes the others immediately. The whole share is ahead of the playhead; nothing behind it is fetched. A small move forward inside the window plays from bytes already in RAM, and a short jump back plays from RAM while the watched pieces have not been evicted yet. A jump past the window moves urgent, readahead and lookahead onto the new spot at once. With one reader on a 256 MiB cache of 2 MiB pieces, the window covers about 240 MiB, most of it ahead. Lookahead receives up to a quarter of the pieces ahead and urgency up to a quarter of the rest. Watched pieces leave the demand and are the first evicted when new pieces need room. Small caches or large pieces can collapse a tier. The cache limit is never raised to meet the Download window sizes. Download metadata preparation expands to at least 16 MiB/two pieces at the head and 8 MiB/two pieces at the tail; RAM-only preparation retains its smaller probes.

Every ten seconds, saturated swarms with waiting candidates prefer recycling leechers idle for at least 15 seconds with no missing pieces on any active forward playback path. Paths include urgent, readahead and lookahead, excluding independent metadata probes, completed pieces and gaps between readers. Without missing playback demand, selection overlap remains the fallback. Seeders and transferring peers remain protected, with a 20-second rotation cooldown and a 60-second low-overlap fallback.

The Pieces grid shows each live playback window as coral **U** (this block, at the playhead) and **N** (the next blocks, fetched in order) badges and colored edges. Existing fills still represent verified/buffered and receiving data. Edges retain proportional coverage inside grouped buckets; the badge shows the highest overlapping priority and tapping shows counts per tier. The legend counts scheduled pieces, including already verified ones, rather than claiming they are being downloaded. Markers move with seeks and disappear when readers close or become idle. These policies have correctness tests; instant startup or faster public-swarm 4K playback is not guaranteed.

**Defaults for a new install.** Ghost Pro mode, Burst-aware buffer, Instant play boost, Play next video, Play this file only, Pause on low battery and Claude optimized seek start on; Stop idle Direct Play starts at 15 minutes. Preload the start stays off so Play opens the player immediately; the engine already requests the first 2 MiB at urgent priority. Ghost Pro keeps Ghost mode, Peer exploration and Fast peer search off until one of them is chosen, which switches Ghost Pro off. A setting the user has changed keeps its saved value; one never touched follows the current default, including after an update.

**Claude optimized seek** is an independent switch in Experimental, on by default, aimed at the time from a seek to the first bytes the player receives. The torrent library normally lets only one peer hold each 16 KiB block request, and moves a request to another peer only when the new peer would hold no more requests than the current one. After a seek this can leave the blocks the player is waiting for on a slow peer that holds only a few requests, while a fast peer with a full pipeline cannot take them. With the mode on, a block of an urgent (PiecePriorityNow) piece outstanding for more than 500 ms moves to a requesting peer that delivered useful data within that time and more recently than the holder. While a playback read is blocked, peers with spare request slots that hold an urgent piece are asked to recompute their requests every 100 ms, so the move does not wait for their pipeline to drain. Stalled reads refresh their urgent request after 3, 6, 12 and 24 seconds instead of 8, 12 and 25, keeping the 45-second total. The eight blocks at the playhead, which the player is frozen on, move after half that age. They also go to fast peers in the first place: after a seek every peer recomputes its requests in no set order, and the first to do so used to take the playhead block even at a few KiB/s. Now a peer leaves it when another connected, unchoking peer with free request slots is at least three times as fast, and that peer is woken to request it at once. In a loopback swarm of 55 peers (half at 40 KiB/s, half at 400 KiB/s), the slowest tenth of seeks into undownloaded video dropped from about 150 ms to about 97 ms to the first bytes; the median stayed at 10–15 ms. These values were chosen by a generative search (`SEEK_SEARCH=1 go test -run TestSeekRuleSearch` in `engine-go/seek_search_test.go`): on a 55-peer loopback swarm at four speeds with 40–400 ms round trips and 256 KiB pieces, it scored rule sets on seek-to-first-64-KiB time (half median, half 90th percentile), kept the best four of twelve, mutated them for four generations, and compared the winners with the previous rules on 60 fresh interleaved seeks: median 107 ms against 138 ms and 90th percentile 217 ms against 283 ms. The winning set also makes only the playhead's 2 MiB Now, with no extra piece. The patch is in `engine-go/patches/torrent/webtor_urgent.go`. Blocks are moved, not duplicated, so download traffic does not increase. It works alongside every other experiment and applies immediately to running playback.

The engine records seek-to-first-byte times in every mode: the time from a jump in the read position to its first delivered bytes, plus stall recoveries. Settings shows the count, median and last seek time under the switch. Toggling the mode starts a new measurement window, so play the same torrent with the mode off, note the median, then turn it on and seek again. A loopback test with one throttled and one fast seed checks correctness in both modes; on loopback both modes seek in about 10 ms, so the speedup on a phone in a public swarm is unmeasured until compared this way.

**Instant seek.** After a seek into a part that is not in RAM yet, Direct Play hands the player each block as soon as a peer delivers it, instead of waiting for the whole piece (4–16 MiB on large videos) and its hash check. Peers are asked for the waited-on piece starting at the seek point, then the rest of it, so the first bytes come from the first block received (the patch is `SetRequestFocus` in `engine-go/patches/torrent/webtor_urgent.go`). Each video's start and end, where players read the index, are still served only after verification: a bad index crashes players, while a rare bad block elsewhere is a brief glitch until the piece is fetched again. Seeks inside the buffer read from RAM without redoing the download window: the window, the library reader and the metadata plan are only updated when the read moves to another piece or another file. Before this, every 32 KiB read re-checked every piece in the window under the torrent lock that incoming blocks also need. A player reopening the file for each seek no longer rebuilds the metadata plan, rescans its folder or rehashes saved pieces. Only the pieces holding the playhead's next 2 MiB are at the top (Now) priority. The rest of Ghost Pro's fast tier, which can be dozens of pieces with a large cache, is one level lower (Next), and both levels are fetched nearest the playhead first rather than rarest first, so the piece being watched is never queued behind the others. Reopening the file on a seek no longer re-adds the saved resume window to RAM when it is already there: each reopen used to allocate and count it again, so the cache counter grew on every seek until the cache looked full and evicted the pieces the player was waiting for (a seek-heavy session could stall outright). The resume window is saved once seeking has settled for 2 seconds, not hashed and fsynced on every seek's close while the next open waited for the same lock, and a reopen with the window already in RAM skips reading and hashing it from disk. A player opens a new HTTP range on every seek; the reader now opens its window at that range instead of at the saved resume position, which sent requests there and moved every window priority twice (about 15% off a seek within the buffer). Tests cover serving delivered blocks before verification, waking a waiting read on the next block, keeping start and end verified only, block order from the playhead and top tiers nearest first, the cache counter across repeated reopens, the settled resume save, and skipping the replan when the same file is reopened.

**Preview frames.** With no player open, the pieces a thumbnail decoder asks for are fetched at the top priority, and selected-episode startup hints are held one level below while it runs. Otherwise a frame inside a later episode waited behind every episode's head and tail (much larger with Ghost Pro) and missed the 10-second extraction deadline. While a player is open, previews stay below playback. A Direct Play preview that needs the network also wakes a session resting under **This block, then the next (2.1.35).** Playback demand is two levels only. The pieces holding the playhead's next 2 MiB are Now; every other piece of the window is Next, and both are fetched nearest the playhead first, in file order, never rarest first. The window's sizes (urgent, readahead, lookahead) only decide how far ahead it reaches. Nothing behind the playhead is fetched. A seek used to leave every peer's request queue full of blocks for the old position: the library never cancelled them, a peer serves requests in the order sent, and a peer with a full queue could not even ask for the new block until the old ones drained. Now an open or a seek, once the new window's priorities are set, cancels every outstanding request outside the Now pieces and refills the peers fastest first (`RefocusRequests` in `engine-go/patches/torrent/webtor_urgent.go`). The Now blocks go to the fastest peers, the rest follows in order from the playhead, and Now blocks may be sent while the cancels still await their reject, so a refocus does not wait a round trip. Each refill scans pieces in file order and stops once it has enough blocks, which also covers Next now. Tests cover the refocus (old queues cleared, playhead block to the fastest peer), in-order fetching of Next pieces ahead of rarer far ones, and no demand behind the playhead.

**Only the latest seek position is fetched (2.1.36).** A player that seeks can leave a reader open at each earlier spot: an HTTP connection it has not closed yet, or a descriptor still waiting on a read. Each kept its window and its library reader's readahead, so after seeking from 1:23 to 4:33 to 7:44 all three spots kept downloading. Now, per file, the reader that last read in the body is the only one fetched for: the others of that file are parked at once (an idle one like an idle probe; one blocked in a read keeps only the piece it waits on). Head and tail probes while opening, and a new descriptor's resume guess, do not displace the playing reader. A parked reader that reads again takes the window back. Closing an earlier reader while another of the same file is open no longer holds its old spot for resume. In 2.1.36 a reader still blocked in a read at the old spot re-published its window every time a block woke it, so old spots kept coming back (three to six at once). From 2.1.37 the rule is enforced where windows are applied: a reader that is not its file's active one publishes no window and no library readahead, whichever path asks. Its blocked read is ended (the player has moved on), and it is parked when the read returns, which also closes the library reader that kept the old piece at top priority. Head and tail probes while opening are still fetched, so a player reopening a file starts even if an old reader lingers. The Pieces grid counts a part-filled piece as receiving only while playback wants it, so a piece left half-done at an earlier spot no longer looks like it is downloading. A test covers three readers seeking 10 → 30 → 50 (before the fix pieces 0, 10 and 30 were all still being fetched), closing the old reader, and a displaced reader reading again.

**One built-in playback mode (2.1.38).** Settings no longer has playback experiments. Playback works like a large video site's player: a seek stops every download for the old place and fetches the new spot first, then the next blocks in order, filling the RAM setting ahead, and nothing behind the playhead is fetched. Two choices replace the old switches. Ghost Pro and optimized seek are always on, and the plain adaptive buffer replaces Burst-aware. Peer exploration, Ghost mode, Faster peer search, Instant play boost and Preload the start are fixed at their defaults, and saved values for them are deleted on start. The choices come from interleaved runs in the engine's simulated swarm (16 delayed, rate-limited seeds, 40–400 ms round trips, 50 seeks per setting; `seek_search_test.go`). Turning optimized seek off made 128 MiB seeks 189 → 288 ms (median) and 516 → 751 ms (p90). Turning Ghost Pro off made medians 143 → 164 ms and 189 → 202 ms. Burst-aware and plain adaptive buffers were within noise of each other. The peer-finding switches cannot be measured on a fixed swarm and stay at their defaults.

Three changes to how blocks are requested ship with it (`engine-go/patches/torrent/webtor_urgent.go`):

- **Request queues sized per peer** (`PipelineRTT` 2). Each peer's round trip is measured from requests sent while nothing else was queued there, and during playback its queue is held to its rate times two round trips. At 128 MiB this made seeks 203 → 163 ms (median) and 537 → 332 ms (p90), with 8% more download; 16 MiB was within noise. Four round trips was worse. Keeping every queue full (refill at half, cancels not counted, `FullPipeline`) made p90 worse (373 → 538 ms, 352 → 702 ms) because a slow peer's long queue holds the playhead block, so it ships off. The limit is fixed for each refill, because the rate moves with the clock and a limit that shrank between the room check and the request crashed the library.
- **The playhead block goes to the peer that answers soonest** (`ArrivalAware`, 1.5×): its round trip plus its queue and the block at its rate, not the fastest downloader, which can be the farthest away. With full reads p90 went 479 → 340 ms and 518 → 339 ms; medians moved within noise.
- **HTTP reads hand over the first block at once** (`ReadSome`). A seek's time was 2 ms scheduling, 94 ms network and 52 ms delivery: a read waited for its whole 64 KiB buffer, three more blocks from other peers, before the player got the first. HTTP reads now return what has arrived (median 202 → 104 ms, p90 479 → 293 ms). Android's file proxy (the content playback method) keeps full reads, because a short read there can be taken for the end of the file.

**Blocks stay where they were asked, and seeds serve in order (2.1.39).** Two changes from block-by-block traces of seeks in the simulated swarm (`TestSeekTrace`, `TestSeekTuningComparison` in `engine-go/seek_search_test.go`):

- **No balance stealing during playback** (`NoBalanceSteal`). The library let any peer that had just received a block take one from a peer with more queued, even a block already on its way, so both copies arrived. Right after a seek one playhead block moved between ten peers within 100 ms. 3–13% of all downloaded data was duplicate. Now, while a video plays, a block moves only when overdue (the urgent steal rule, 250 ms at the playhead). Duplicates fell to about 0.1%, and a sustained cold download went from 2.26 to 2.52 MiB/s at 16 MiB of RAM and from 2.61 to 3.64 MiB/s at 48 MiB. Download while seeking rose 25–32%, and seek times stayed equal (150 seeks each at 128 MiB: median 144 vs 144 ms, p90 286 vs 278 ms). Keeping the rule for the playhead piece's blocks brought the duplicates back (13%), so it applies to every block. The playhead focus is cleared when the last reader of a torrent closes, so a download that goes on afterwards uses the plain rules.
- **Our seeding serves requests in the order they arrived.** The library read and sent a peer's queued requests in random order (Go map iteration), so a streaming peer's urgent block could go out last. It now serves the oldest first, as libtorrent and Transmission do, which also makes the simulated seeds realistic.

Tried and dropped, each measured against the shipped rules in the same runs:

- Asking a second peer for the playhead blocks (a hedge, as in tail-latency hedged requests), at once or only when overdue: no faster seeks, 1–5% more duplicate data.
- Keeping each peer's request limit across a seek, fully or at 2 or 4. Each block of a full read then no longer goes to a different peer: 128 KiB reads got faster (median 197 → 153 ms), but 32 KiB reads got slower (110 → 130–181 ms) and p90 got worse. Its extra download appeared only right after seeks, not in a sustained download.
- A TCP Westwood-style rate estimate (recent delivery rate over 200 ms windows instead of the lifetime average) for peer choice and queue sizing: within noise, slightly slower.
- Counting blocks still in flight for cancelled requests when picking the soonest peer, and pinning only the playhead blocks: no change.

The per-peer queue (`PipelineRTT` 2, rate × two round trips) is already BBR's window: bottleneck rate times minimum round trip times a gain of 2.

**Back to the 2.1.38 rules (2.1.40).** On a real swarm, seeking in 2.1.39 was slower than in 2.1.38, which the simulated swarm did not show. Both 2.1.39 changes and the focus clearing are undone: `NoBalanceSteal` stays as an option, off by default, and peer requests are served in map order again. Two fixed waits in the app are gone: the 750 ms pause before a network change reaches the engine, and the 180 ms debounce before a typed or pasted magnet starts loading. The seek and read path has no fixed sleeps: reads wake when their piece completes. Its only fixed times are the urgent steal age (500 ms, 250 ms at the playhead) and the 100 ms nudge while a read waits.

**HTTP stream is the default (2.1.41).** On the phone it seeks faster than the content URI, whose file proxy splits reads into 32–128 KiB requests that each wait for every block. A saved choice is kept.

**2.1.40's HTTP stream again (2.1.42).** 2.1.41 also changed the stream: each read flushed at once, reads of up to 256 KiB instead of 32 KiB, and per-extension video types. HTTP playback on the phone felt slower than in 2.1.40, so all three are undone. The flush bought almost nothing: Go's server already writes a response body straight to the socket after its first 512 bytes. `TestSeekHTTPComparison` (`SEEK_SEARCH=http`) compares the writers in the simulated swarm. Over HTTP with 16 simulated seeds, 36 seeks and 12 cold 8 MiB streams each: reads of up to 256 KiB streamed at 2.14–2.19 MiB/s against 2.31 for 2.1.40 and reached 1 MiB later (median 800–836 vs 765 ms); first byte and 64 KiB were equal (95–100 ms and 144 ms).

**Web seeds follow a seek at once (2.1.43).** Web seeds (`ws=` in a magnet, `url-list` in a .torrent) are HTTP servers holding the file. The library replanned their requests only every 5 s or when a request ended, so after a seek, and at playback start, they kept fetching the old position for up to 5 s. A piece that becomes urgent now replans them at once (`TestWebseedStartsAtAnUrgentPieceAtOnce`: under 0.1 s, against 7 s without). Each request covers a 64 MiB-aligned slice from its first missing block, so a seek within the slice being fetched still waits for that request to reach it.

**Seek log, staying unchoked, and a larger starting window (2.1.44).**

- **Seek log on the phone.** Every HTTP playback request adds a line to `/sdcard/Android/data/webtor.app/files/seek-log.csv` (pull with `adb pull`; 2 MiB, then one `.1` backup): time, app version, the first 8 hex digits of the infohash, file index, `open` or `range`, offset (MiB and %), milliseconds to the first byte, to 1 MiB and to 2 MiB (about what a player buffers before resuming), total time and bytes, whether the first piece was already in RAM, connected peers, peers letting us download, and the download rate. Twice the simulated swarm and the phone disagreed (2.1.39, 2.1.41); seek changes are now judged on these lines.
- **Staying unchoked.** The library declared interest only in peers holding a piece it would request now, during playback the window around the playhead, so a peer with only other parts of the file never heard we were interested and kept us choked. A seek there waited to be let in, up to the peer's next rechoke (about 10 s in common clients). While a file plays, we now stay interested in every peer with a piece of it that we lack (`SetPlaybackInterest`, `TestStaysInterestedInPeersWithAnyPartOfThePlayingFile`). The simulated seeds never choke, so only the phone log can show the effect.
- **`MinPipeline` 8.** Each peer's playback window is its bandwidth-delay product times two plus 8 requests, up from 2, like TCP's larger initial window. Time to 2 MiB after a seek over HTTP, 64 MiB cache, same-run medians: 939 vs 1062 ms in the first test, then 1118 vs 1120 and 1072 vs 1183 ms in two confirmation runs of 48 seeks each; stream rate and p90 equal. Not a confirmed gain, no measured cost.

Measured and not shipped (time to 2 MiB against the shipped rules in the same run): topping peers up continuously as blocks arrive (`RefillAt` 0.5 and 0.9: 1299–1363 vs 1225 ms, stream 1.95–1.98 vs 2.14 MiB/s), keeping the request limit across a seek (`FullPipeline`), spreading the playhead over 32 or 128 blocks, `PipelineRTT` 3, and `MinPipeline` 16. RAM cache, same run: 16 MiB 1134 ms and 2.27 MiB/s, 64 MiB 911 ms and 2.23, 128 MiB 898 ms and 2.68, 256 MiB 850 ms and 2.73.

**Head-of-line rescue and A/B tests on the phone (2.1.45).** The first phone seek log (2.1.44, 22 seeks to cold spots on a 3 GB and a 1.7 GB torrent, 4–50 peers): first byte median 242 ms, p90 459 ms, but 1 MiB median 1454 ms, p90 2777 ms, and in 7 of 17 seeks 1 MiB and 2 MiB arrived within 20 ms of each other. The blocks after the playhead had arrived; the stream waited on one slow block before them. The simulated seeds deliver steadily, so the simulation never showed it.

- **Head-of-line rescue** (`HOLRescue`, `HOLAfterMs` 300). While a read waits, the nudger finds the first block after the playhead that has not arrived. Once its request is older than 300 ms, or twice its peer's round trip if that is longer, it goes to the unchoked peer with a free slot expected to deliver it soonest, if that is sooner than the holder (`TestHeadOfLineBlockMovesToASoonerPeerWhenOverdue`). A refilling peer takes unclaimed blocks first and stops at its limit, so the steal rules never reached such a block.
- **A/B on the phone.** With the seek log on, the app turns on `seekExperiment`: each HTTP range request runs with `MinPipeline` 8 or 0 and the rescue on or off, picked at random and independently, and the log adds `variant` (`m8+r`, `m8-r`, `m0+r`, `m0-r`) and `rescues` (blocks moved during the request). One session compares all four on the same swarm. A log with the 2.1.44 columns is moved to `seek-log.csv.old`.

**Rescue every stuck block; 2 MiB to the soonest peers (2.1.46).** The 2.1.45 A/B, 36 seeks to cold spots: with the rescue on (17), 1 MiB median 2694 ms and 2 MiB 2759 ms; off (19), 2719 and 2836 ms. It fired in 15 of 17 seeks, about 3 blocks a seek. Several blocks were stuck in a row, and moving only the first, then waiting for the next to be overdue, chained the waits. `MinPipeline` 8 made no difference on the phone either (1 MiB 2719 vs 2694 ms), nor across five simulated runs (−12% to +7%), so it is back to 0.

- The rescue now moves every overdue block of the first 2 MiB after the playhead in one pass, up to 16, each to the peer expected to deliver it soonest counting the blocks just given to it.
- The A/B now picks `FocusBlocks` 8 or 128 (the blocks after the playhead sent to the soonest peers: 128 KiB or the whole 2 MiB) and the rescue on or off; the log names them `f8+r`, `f8-r`, `f128+r`, `f128-r`.

**Blocks as they arrive in download playback; urgent pieces hashed first (2.1.47).** The 2.1.46 A/B, 39 seeks: the rescue moved 12 blocks a seek and did not help (1 MiB 2414 vs 2341 ms, 2 MiB 2814 vs 2447 ms); it stays in the code, off. `FocusBlocks` 128 was slightly faster to 1 MiB (2258 vs 2414 ms; 2062 vs 2393 ms with 55+ peers). In every log 1 and 2 MiB kept arriving within milliseconds of each other. Direct Play is off by default, and download playback read only verified pieces: the library reader was never made responsive. The simulated swarm had always played in Direct Play, so it never showed this.

- **Unverified reads in download playback** (`applyResponsiveLocked`). When on, the library reader returns received blocks before their piece is verified, except in the head (5 MiB) and tail (3 MiB) of the file, where players read the container index (`TestUnverifiedReadsSkipTheStartAndEndOfTheFile`). A bad block from a peer is then a brief glitch before the hash check rejects the piece.
- **Urgent pieces hashed first.** The hash queue took pieces in index order, so a background download's pieces below the playhead were hashed before the one a read waited on (`TestPlaybackPiecesAreHashedFirst`).
- **A/B:** `u` (blocks as they arrive) or `v` (verified pieces) and `f8` or `f128`, named `u-f8`, `u-f128`, `v-f8`, `v-f128`. The log adds `mode` (`direct` or `download`) and `piece_kib`.
- The simulated swarm can play in download mode (`SEEK_MODE=download`, file priority none so only playback fetches) with other piece sizes (`SEEK_PIECE_KB`).

**Final rules, log off (2.1.48).** The 2.1.47 log showed both test torrents in Direct Play (4 MiB and 512 KiB pieces), so the `u`/`v` switch did not apply; its spread (1 MiB 2284 vs 2076 ms) is the noise of these phone comparisons, about ±10%. `FocusBlocks` 128 tied on 1 MiB (2210 vs 2233 ms) and was slower to 2 MiB (2838 vs 2275 ms; in 2.1.46, 2814 vs 2540 ms). No variant beat the shipped rules on the phone, so they stay: `FocusBlocks` 8, `MinPipeline` 0, rescue off, verified pieces in download playback. Kept from this series: HTTP playback by default, staying interested in peers that hold part of the playing file, web seeds replanned on urgent pieces, urgent pieces hashed first. The app no longer turns on the seek log or the A/B test; the engine keeps both, off, for later measurements (`seekLogPath`, `seekExperiment`). On the phone a cold seek in Direct Play now shows its first byte in about 230 ms and reaches 1 MiB in about 2.1 s.


**Stop idle Direct Play**; previews served from RAM or disk do not. Reads wake a resting session without waiting for the torrent lock: pause, remove and preview close hold that lock while joining reader I/O, so a read waiting for it would deadlock the engine (covered by a regression test). Tests cover both: a mid-episode preview of the fourth episode of a pack from a 2 MiB/s seed, and a preview of a resting Direct Play session.

**Play next warm-up** loads the start of the next episode before the current one ends. With **Play next video** on, `/play` names the video Play next would open after this one (natural episode order, selected and not already complete) as `nextFileIndex`. When a reader of the current file reads past 90% of it — outside the opening head/tail probes and after any scrub settles — the engine raises the next episode's head and tail at High priority, below every playback window. In Direct Play they are the same 2 MiB head and 2 MiB tail kept by the metadata cache, usually saved already; they stay out of RAM eviction until saved to disk, so playing the next episode starts from verified bytes even with no peers yet. Downloads use the 5 MiB head and 3 MiB tail. The next `/play` replaces the plan. `/torrent/{id}` reports `nextPreload` (`fileIndex`, `active`, `ready`), and Download details marks the episode row "Up next". Tests cover the trigger (no start from probes, mid-episode reads or a raw scrub), durability, the separate budget when the current episode fills its own, and a play of the next episode with every peer disconnected.

**Stop idle Direct Play** (Settings → Playback; Off, 5, 15 or 30 minutes, default 15) limits what an unattended Direct Play session uses. After 2 minutes without a playback read (or the chosen limit, if shorter), the engine *rests* the torrent: it saves the dialable addresses of its connected peers, then sets its connection limit to 0, closing every peer connection and opening no new ones. RAM pieces, the startup cache and the torrent record stay. A session does not rest while an open player's urgent or readahead window still misses pieces, so a paused player keeps filling its buffer first. Lookahead is outside that wait: it is the first RAM dropped when the cache is tight, and requiring it would keep peers connected after the player's own buffer is full. `/play` wakes a resting session immediately, while the player launches; otherwise the next playback read wakes it before waiting for data: the saved peers are re-added and the previous connection limit is restored, so a paused external player resumes from RAM and reconnects in the background. Once a resting session has gone the full limit without playback and has no player reader open, the engine reports `idleDropReady`; Android then drops it exactly like the **Drop** button in Download details: the torrent leaves the engine, peers disconnect and its RAM is freed, while the library entry, resume position, startup cache and saved peer addresses remain. Play starts it again and dials the saved peers at once instead of waiting for trackers and DHT; only deleting the torrent forgets them. Opening a dropped Direct Play torrent's file list starts it in the background (unless transfers are stopped, the battery pause is on or the network policy blocks data), so peers are connected by the time an episode is chosen. An open but paused player is never dropped, only rested. Pausing the transfer or turning the setting off wakes the session. `/torrent/{id}` reports `playbackReaders`, `resting` and `idleDropReady` for Direct Play torrents; `/settings` accepts `idleDropMinutes` (0–240). A loopback test covers rest, drop-readiness, the open-player guard, wake-and-read with correct bytes, pause and turning the setting off.

To compare the experiments, keep the torrent, player, RAM limit, peer cap, Instant play boost and network the same. Start with Adaptive, Burst-aware, Peer exploration and Ghost off, then enable each separately. Compare time to first playback, stall count and duration, transfer speed and memory over several runs. Give Peer exploration at least two minutes with a full connection pool and waiting candidates. Give Ghost at least a minute with spare connection slots, waiting candidates and Fast peer search off. Network and swarm changes can outweigh differences between policies.

The Direct Play pieces grid shows current RAM residency. Verified pieces in RAM fill green, resident pieces still arriving show amber, and absent or evicted pieces are hollow. A saved startup copy on disk does not keep an evicted RAM block filled. Larger torrents group several pieces in each block; the filled fraction follows the resident piece count. The engine reuses one compressed grid snapshot per torrent until the buffer changes and signals these changes through the existing UI event clock.

Direct Play waits for verified storage pieces through cancellation-aware events, copying each piece before eviction can replace its bytes. Large range reads advance the playhead window while reading. Reader shutdown cancels and joins active torrent I/O before closing its storage cursor. The pinned anacrolix v1.61.0 dependency includes only the upstream [peer-writer wakeup fix](https://github.com/anacrolix/torrent/commit/aa71e8d2f5c439257c0313919d671fd087347233) and its regression test; see [patch provenance](../engine-go/patches/torrent/WEBTOR_PATCH.md).

Closing the external player retains one roughly 2 MiB resume range (rounded to torrent pieces) for up to 90 seconds, within the existing cache budget. Reopened readers start their scheduling window near the recent body read; metadata probes cannot replace that hint. When a compatible player returns its position and duration, playback also prefetches an estimated byte position before launch. The player still controls the actual timestamp seek, including variable-bitrate media. Pause, removal and shutdown release the hold. Completed downloads open locally without waiting on unrelated saved-data checks, and repeated playback skips redundant MediaStore metadata refreshes.

Direct Play library entries load paused with no assumed RAM progress. Reopening the app does not restore or prebuffer them automatically. **Clear cache** deletes imported temporary torrents and inactive startup caches while preserving active playback and download scratch storage. Deleting a Direct Play entry with the erase action also removes its startup cache.

External playback uses Android content URI grants. Compatible MX-style players receive a naturally ordered selected-video playlist and matching selected subtitle sidecars. VLC also receives its subtitle-location extra. Resume positions are saved only when the external player returns a supported numeric position; playlist returns must identify the episode before its position is recorded. Other players may ignore these extras or keep their own bookmarks. Video playback buttons always show **Play**, including when a saved position is passed to the player. The episode three-dot menu and separate play-from-start action have been removed. Subtitles must be selected with the video; embedded subtitle controls remain in the external player.

**Settings → Playback method** selects **Standard** or **HTTP stream (experimental)** as the saved method for Play on incomplete videos and Direct Play. HTTP hands the player the existing loopback range stream; Standard uses the content URI. Completed downloads keep the direct local-file path. Episode rows keep one compact playback button visible. See the [HTTP comparison notes](HTTP_PLAYER_EXPLORATION.md) for caching, lifecycle behavior, validation and device-test limits.

Validation covers startup corruption, RAM eviction without fabricated bytes, asynchronous close, active-cache preservation, episode switching, idle reader wakeup and stalled-read retry. A loopback peer test downloads and seeks through a 40 MiB payload while enforcing a 16 MiB video cache. These are correctness checks, not measurements of first-play latency on a phone or a public swarm.

## 2.1.49: request limit no longer collapses while playing

While a read waits, claude-seek mode nudges peers every 100 ms. Upstream sets
a peer's request limit to twice the blocks its last update added, so a nudge
of a busy peer (adding 0–1 blocks) dropped it to one request: during playback
only the next block downloaded, and stopping playback restored full speed.
The limit now follows the whole outstanding queue (`PeakMode: PeakWholeQueue`,
test `TestTopUpOfABusyPeerKeepsItsRequestLimit`). Simulated 16-peer swarm,
12 MiB cold reads, 6 runs each: 2.38 → 3.31 MiB/s (+39%).

Codex's other read-ahead changes: refill at half (`RefillAt` 0.5) and
unverified body reads by default stay off, both slower to seek in earlier
measurements. Its read-rate sampler and the Rust received-range merge are
kept; the merge matches the old sort-and-merge (`TestReceivedRangesMergeLikeASortedMerge`,
Go and `rust` builds). Seek times for 2.1.49 were not measured before release.

## 2.1.50: hedged head-of-line requests, off

From TorrServer's fork (anacrolix v1.2 request strategy 3): an overdue block
is asked of a second peer without cancelling the first. `SeekTuning.Hedge`
sends one duplicate request per overdue block of the 2 MiB after the playhead
(after `HOLAfterMs`, or twice the holder's round trip) to the soonest peer
with a free slot, outside the library's one-owner bookkeeping; the later copy
is cancelled, and its reject or late block is accepted, not taken for a
protocol error (`TestOverdueHeadOfLineBlockIsHedgedOnASecondPeer`). Off by
default: not yet measured. TorrServer's priority watchdog was not taken: here
only the playback coordinator sets piece priorities, so none can be lost.

## 2.1.51–2.1.52: experimental switches

Settings → Playback, all off by default; switching one restarts the on-screen
seek times so on and off can be compared.

- Hedged requests (2.1.51): `SeekTuning.Hedge`, see 2.1.50.
- Slow peers step aside: `SeekTuning.SlowPeerYield`, WebTorrent and
  torrent-stream's speed ranker. A peer under 48 KiB/s skips a playback piece
  (Next or higher) that faster unchoked peers holding it would finish in the
  time it needs for its own queue; at most 10 pieces per refill.
  `TestSlowPeerLeavesAPlaybackPieceToFasterPeers`.
- Drop peers that won't share: `Torrent.WebtorDropChokers`, their
  CHOKE_TIMEOUT. Once a second, up to two peers that kept us choked for 5 s
  while we were interested are dropped, if more peers wait for a connection
  than twice the free slots. `TestPeerThatKeepsUsChokedIsDropped`.

None was measured on a swarm before release.
